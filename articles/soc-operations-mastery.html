Engineering a World-Class SOC:
Frameworks, Metrics & Methodology

What actually makes a security team great — not the number of tools or people, but how well it detects, responds to, and learns from attacks. A practical guide to measuring and improving a security operations team, in plain language.

2023 – 2024 10 min read Joe Smith-Essang SOC Strategy & Architecture

Context

This article distils observations and methodology from over five years of security operations leadership, including SOC capability maturity assessments, IR engagements, and keynote presentations at industry forums. Content presented at the Second Annual Managed Security Services Nigeria Forum (MSS Forum, February 2023) is incorporated here in synthesised form.

In Plain Terms

A SOC (Security Operations Centre) is the team that watches over an organization's computers and networks around the clock, looking for signs of attack and responding when something's wrong — think of it as the security guards and alarm room for a company's digital world. Two numbers come up a lot: MTTD (Mean Time to Detect) is how long it takes to notice an attack, and MTTR (Mean Time to Respond) is how long it takes to deal with it. Lower is better for both.

What Makes a Security Team Great

Most organizations have a security team. Far fewer have a genuinely effective one. The difference isn't how many people or tools they have — it's how well they work together toward a clear goal. Three things set the best teams apart:

  • They go looking for trouble. Instead of only waiting for alarms, they actively search for attackers who may already be hiding — so they catch problems sooner.
  • They follow a tested plan. Every type of incident has a written, rehearsed step-by-step guide. Under pressure, analysts follow a proven process instead of improvising, saving their judgement for the genuinely tricky cases.
  • They learn from every miss. When something slips through or a false alarm wastes time, that lesson feeds straight back in — so the team gets measurably better every few months.

Assessment Application

SOC CMM assessments were conducted for multiple enterprise clients across the banking, insurance, and energy sectors. In each case, the assessment identified specific, actionable gaps (typically in the Process and Services domains) that were translated into a structured improvement roadmap with measurable milestones.

The Four Numbers Worth Tracking

A good security team proves its performance with numbers rather than claims. These four are the clearest signs of how well a team is doing:

MTTD

<1h

Mean Time to Detect — how long until you notice an attack. The best teams aim for under an hour. Getting there is about better-quality alerts, not simply more of them.

MTTR

<4h

Mean Time to Respond — how long from noticing an attack to shutting it down. Having a rehearsed, step-by-step plan is the biggest thing that speeds this up.

False Positive Rate

<5%

How many alarms turn out to be nothing. Too many false alarms exhaust the team and make them stop trusting alerts. Keep this low by continually fine-tuning what triggers an alert.

Coverage

100%

How much of your environment you're actually watching. Anything you're not monitoring is a blind spot — and an attacker will find it before you do.

Test Your Numbers with Real Exercises

It's easy to fool yourself with metrics, so test them for real. In a “purple team” exercise, a friendly attacking team (red) and your defending team (blue) work side by side: the attackers try known techniques and tell the defenders what they're doing, so you can see exactly what your team catches and what it misses.

In practice, these exercises repeatedly showed that teams' own estimates of how fast they detect and respond were three to five times too optimistic. That gap between what you think you can do and what you actually can do is the most dangerous blind spot a security team has.

How to Write a Response Plan That Works

A “playbook” is a step-by-step guide for handling a specific type of incident. A good one is worth its weight in gold during a crisis; a bad one just gathers dust. Five things separate a useful playbook from a document nobody opens:

SCENARIO-SPECIFIC

Each playbook covers one specific type of incident (ransomware, email fraud, a stolen password, a website being flooded offline). One-size-fits-all playbooks fail under pressure, because real incidents are specific. A ransomware plan and a phishing plan can look similar on paper but need very different steps.

DECISION TREE DRIVEN

A good playbook asks clear yes/no questions and tells you what to do for each answer (“Is a second login step turned on? Yes → do this. No → do that.”). This removes guesswork and speeds things up when the clock is ticking. Some tools can even automate the routine first steps.

REGULARLY TESTED

Playbooks must be validated in tabletop exercises at least quarterly and updated after every significant incident. A playbook that has never been executed under simulated conditions is theoretical documentation, not an operational asset.

REGULATOR-AWARE

For regulated industries (banking, healthcare, critical infrastructure), the playbook must integrate regulatory notification requirements with specific timelines. The incident response workflow and the compliance workflow must be one document, not two separate processes running in parallel.

ROLE-ASSIGNED

Every step names exactly who does it — a specific analyst, the team manager, legal, or the security chief. When it's unclear who owns an action during a live incident, things get missed. A good playbook removes that doubt in advance.

Going Looking for Hidden Attackers

“Threat hunting” means actively searching for attackers who may already be inside but haven't set off any alarm — rather than only reacting when an alarm goes off. The approach is simple: form a hunch about how an attacker might behave, then go look for evidence of it in your data.

Hunting Through Publicly Available Information

One of the most useful and most overlooked hunts uses only publicly available information — checking what your own organization has accidentally exposed to the open internet. In one engagement, this simple approach found serious leaks before attackers could use them:

FINDING — PASSWORDS LEFT IN PUBLIC CODE

Searching public code-sharing sites for the organization's name and its developers' usernames turned up real passwords and access keys that staff had accidentally left in code they'd posted publicly from their personal accounts. Anyone who found them could have logged straight into the company's live cloud systems.

FINDING — INTERNAL SYSTEM DETAILS EXPOSED

A tool developers use to test their systems had been shared publicly by mistake (the default setting made it visible to everyone). It contained login keys and a full map of the company's internal systems — effectively handing an attacker a ready-made instruction manual for breaking in.

Lesson for Security Teams

The outside tools your developers use (code-sharing sites, testing tools, cloud storage) are part of what an attacker can see. Regularly check these places for anything your organization has exposed by accident. Make it a routine monthly job, not a one-off.

The Four Steps of a Hunt

  • Form a hunch. Based on how attackers are known to behave — especially those targeting your industry — decide what you're going to look for.
  • Gather the data. Work out what records you'd need to check the hunch. If that data doesn't exist, that's a finding in itself: you have a blind spot to fix.
  • Analyse it. Look for things that stand out from normal, everyday activity — not just known bad signatures.
  • Act and improve. If you find something, treat it as an incident. If you find nothing, ask why — and, where you can, build an automatic alert so the same thing gets caught next time without a manual hunt.

Where to Spend Your Security Budget First

When clients ask what to fund first, my answer is consistent: get these three basics right before spending on anything fancier. They stop the most common attacks and make everything else work better.

1. Modern Protection on Every Device

Tools that watch each computer for suspicious behaviour (called EDR) give you eyes on the place attacks most often play out. Every device needs to be covered — the ones you're not watching are exactly where an attacker will hide. This is the foundation everything else depends on.

2. Control Who Can Log In and What They Can Do

Most serious incidents involve a stolen password or misused access. Requiring a second login step, giving people only the access they need, reviewing that access regularly, and keeping tight control of powerful admin accounts prevents more incidents than almost any detection tool can catch.

3. Build Security Into Your Software

Many breaches start in an organization's own applications — through flaws that were never checked before the software went live. Testing code for security problems automatically, as it's written, catches these early, when they're cheap to fix. The goal is to make security part of how developers work, rather than a gate a stretched security team polices at the end.

Using a Recognized Standard to Stay Organized

ISO
27001

A Structured Way to Manage Security

ISO 27001 is an internationally recognized standard for managing information security in an organized, provable way. For a large bank, I helped put it in place together with two related standards — one for keeping the business running through disruptions, and one for running IT services well. Combining them into a single system removed duplicated effort, cut down on audits, and gave the security, resilience, and IT teams one shared way of talking about risk. The initial review found 68 gaps to fix, with a 14-month plan to get certified.

The most common gap these reviews uncover isn't a missing security tool — it's missing paperwork. Organizations often have the right protections in place but can't prove it, because they never kept records. The lesson that comes up again and again: build record-keeping into everyday work, rather than scrambling to assemble evidence at audit time.

Thinking Like an Attacker at the Design Stage

“Threat modelling” simply means asking “how could someone attack this?” while you're still designing a system — when fixing a flaw costs a fraction of what it costs after launch. Two well-known checklists help structure that thinking:

STRIDE MODEL

  • Spoofing — Can an attacker impersonate another user or component?
  • Tampering — Can data be modified without detection?
  • Repudiation — Can actions be denied without an audit trail?
  • Information Disclosure — Can sensitive data be exposed?
  • Denial of Service — Can the system be made unavailable?
  • Elevation of Privilege — Can a user gain unauthorized access?

DREAD SCORING

  • Damage potential — How severe is the impact?
  • Reproducibility — How easily can the attack be repeated?
  • Exploitability — What is the technical barrier?
  • Affected users — How many users are impacted?
  • Discoverability — How easy is the vulnerability to find?
  • Each dimension scored 1-10 → Average = risk priority

The Anatomy of a High-Performing SOC Manager

Technology and process provide the foundation, but the SOC manager defines the ceiling. Based on direct experience leading and coaching SOC teams, these qualities consistently differentiate elite SOC managers from capable ones:

Real Technical Know-How

They genuinely understand the technology they oversee — that's how they earn the respect of their analysts. At the same time, they need broad knowledge across incident response, forensics, and cloud, not just deep expertise in one area.

Business Fluency

Can translate technical findings into business risk language that resonates with a CFO or board member. Knows how to build a security budget case, not just a threat report.

Calm Under Pressure

Major incidents are chaotic. The SOC manager's composure sets the tone for the entire team's effectiveness. Panic is contagious; so is composed, methodical authority.

Continuous Learning Culture

Builds a team where knowledge is shared, post-incident reviews are blameless learning sessions, and analyst upskilling is treated as a priority, not a distraction from operations.

Decisions Based on Data

Makes staffing, tool, and process decisions based on the numbers — detection speed, response speed, case volumes, coverage — not gut feeling. Tracks whether things are actually improving over time.

Stakeholder Management

Maintains relationships across IT, legal, compliance, communications, and executive teams before incidents occur. The IR call is not the first conversation with these stakeholders.

Recommendations for Building SOC Capability

Assess Before You Spend

Score your team against the maturity model before hiring more people or buying more tools. It shows which areas are genuinely weak versus which just look weak because of poor record-keeping. Spending without assessing tends to fix the wrong gaps.

Write Plans for the Threats You Actually Face

Create response plans for the incidents most likely to hit your industry: ransomware, email fraud, insider misuse, stolen passwords. A specific plan gets you back on your feet far faster than a vague, general one.

Test How Well You Really Detect Attacks

Run friendly attack exercises using known techniques, and measure how much you actually catch and how fast. Assume your own estimates are three to five times too optimistic until you've proven otherwise.

Actually Act on Threat Information

Threat information you receive but never use is just reading material. Have a clear process to review it and turn it into new alerts, hunts, or patch priorities within a day of receiving it.

Build Security Into How You Develop Software

If developers can put code live with no security check, your risk keeps growing. Add automatic security testing to the development process and make sure changes get a second pair of eyes before release.

Plan Improvement Over Several Years

Use the maturity model to score where you are, decide where you want to be in a few years, and map out a year-by-year plan to get there. This also gives you a solid case for ongoing security funding.

Key Takeaways

  • Understand your gaps before buying tools. A maturity assessment shows you what's actually wrong. Buying tools without one usually solves the wrong problem.
  • Rehearse your response plans regularly. A plan that's never been tested under pressure is a risk, not a safeguard.
  • Actively hunt for hidden attackers. For mature teams, going looking finds intruders that automated alerts consistently miss.
  • Check what you've exposed by accident. Searching public sites for your own leaked data routinely finds serious problems your internal tools can't see. Make it a monthly habit.
  • Get the basics right in order: protect every device first, then control who can log in and what they can do, then build security into your software. That sequence gives the most protection for the money.
  • The team's leader sets the ceiling. Invest in your security manager's skills and judgement as seriously as you invest in tools — a great one lifts the whole team.
