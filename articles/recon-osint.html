Offensive Security Tradecraft: Inside a Professional Reconnaissance Operation

A practitioner's walkthrough of how ethical hackers systematically map an organization's attack surface — from passive OSINT through infrastructure enumeration to internal situational awareness — before a single exploit is fired.

Joe Smith-Essang Discipline: Penetration Testing / Red Team Methodology, 2021 12 min read

Why Reconnaissance Defines the Engagement

In professional penetration testing, reconnaissance is not a preliminary step — it is the foundation on which the entire engagement rests. The quality of intelligence gathered during reconnaissance directly determines the precision and impact of subsequent attack phases. A rushed reconnaissance phase produces a shallow assessment; thorough reconnaissance uncovers the asymmetric entry points that no automated scanner will ever find.

This article is drawn from a detailed reconnaissance methodology framework developed and refined across enterprise-grade black-box penetration testing engagements. Nothing here is instruction for unauthorized use. All techniques described are used exclusively within the scope of authorized security assessments, governed by a Letter of Engagement (LoE) that defines legal boundaries before any testing begins.

Authorization First
Every technique described in this article requires explicit written authorization from the target organization. A Letter of Engagement defining scope, testing window, emergency contacts, and authorized activities is a non-negotiable prerequisite. Testing without authorization is illegal and unethical — full stop.

Black-Box vs White-Box Engagements

For the reconnaissance methodology described here, the engagement model is black-box: the tester simulates an external attacker with no prior knowledge of the target's internal systems, architecture, or credentials. The client defines scope as "the entirety of their external touchpoints" — which means the tester must first discover those touchpoints before assessing them. This is precisely where reconnaissance methodology matters most.

Note-Taking: The Most Underrated Practice in Penetration Testing

Before any tool is run, a disciplined note-taking system must be in place. Reconnaissance generates enormous volumes of data — domains, IPs, employee names, technology fingerprints, exposed credentials, configuration details — and the ability to retrieve, cross-reference, and act on that data later determines the quality of the final report. There is no right tool; there is only consistent, structured practice. Organized intelligence is exploitable; disorganized intelligence is noise.

Phase 1 — Passive Reconnaissance: Building Intelligence Without Touching the Target

Passive reconnaissance involves gathering intelligence from publicly available sources — without sending a single packet to the target's infrastructure. It is entirely legal and produces intelligence that surprises most organizations about what they expose to the open internet.

Corporate Websites, LinkedIn & Job Postings

An organization's own digital presence is one of the richest OSINT sources available. Through systematic analysis of corporate websites, social media profiles, and job postings, a skilled analyst can extract:

  • Email naming conventions — first.last@company.com vs flast@company.com reveals the format for crafting targeted phishing lures or credential spraying lists
  • Technology stack details — technical job descriptions frequently enumerate specific products in use ("experience with Palo Alto NGFWs", "proficiency in Splunk SIEM") revealing the defensive environment
  • Key personnel — names and roles of IT and security staff are valuable for social engineering and for understanding the organizational security structure
  • Partnerships and integrations — mentions of third-party providers and cloud platforms indicate potential supply chain entry points
Defender Insight
Review your own organization's job postings from an attacker's perspective. Ask: does this listing reveal which security tools we use? Does it disclose our cloud provider? Technical specificity in job postings is a competitive necessity but also an intelligence gift to adversaries. Consider how much technology detail is truly necessary.

Google Dorking

Google's advanced search operators enable highly targeted enumeration of an organization's internet-exposed assets and related domains. By searching for a target's copyright notice, terms of service text, or privacy policy language, testers can discover related web properties that the organization may not actively maintain or monitor.

site:target.com ext:pdf inurl:"login" site:target.com "copyright 2024 TargetOrg" -site:target.com intitle:"index of" site:target.com

The Google Hacking Database (GHDB) maintains a catalogue of dorks targeting specific vulnerability classes — exposed admin panels, configuration files, backup files, and credential stores — that can be searched against any target within scope.

GitHub Dorking: Leaked Source Code & Credentials

As engineering teams grow, the probability of a developer accidentally pushing sensitive material to a public repository increases. GitHub dorking involves searching public repositories for an organization's name, domain, or known product names to find accidentally leaked secrets.

org:TargetOrg "password" extension:config org:TargetOrg "api_key" language:python "target.com" "secret" pushed:>2023-01-01
Common Finding Pattern
In a significant proportion of enterprise engagements, at least one developer has committed credentials to a public repository — often database connection strings, API keys, or internal IP addresses in configuration files that were intended to remain private. These findings are high-impact because they require no exploitation; the attacker simply reads the exposed secret.

Phase 2 — Asset Discovery: Mapping the Attack Surface

Asset discovery translates passive intelligence into a structured map of the target's internet-facing infrastructure. The goal is to identify every IP, domain, subdomain, and cloud asset within scope — including assets the organization may have forgotten it owns.

ASN Enumeration

Autonomous System Numbers (ASNs) are identifiers assigned to large network blocks. Enumerating an organization's ASNs reveals their entire IP address space — including legacy infrastructure and cloud presence.

amass intel --asn <ASN_NUMBER> # Hurricane Electric lookup: http://bgp.he.net # crt.sh certificate transparency search for additional IP ranges

A critical caveat: because modern organizations use shared cloud infrastructure (AWS, Azure, GCP), ASN enumeration does not capture cloud-hosted assets, which may appear under the cloud provider's ASN. Certificate transparency logs via crt.sh fill this gap by revealing hostnames regardless of hosting provider.

Reverse WHOIS

Standard WHOIS lookups reveal the registrant of a single domain. Reverse WHOIS queries invert this — given a registrant name, email, or phone number, find all domains registered to that entity. This surfaces related domains the organization may not list publicly, including acquired subsidiaries, test environments, and legacy domains.

Subdomain Enumeration

A primary domain like target.com may host hundreds of subdomains, each representing a distinct web application or service — many of which are less carefully maintained than the main site. Subdomain enumeration uses three complementary techniques:

Passive

Subdomain Scraping

Amass / Subfinder: Parse multiple public intelligence sources (certificate transparency, DNS records, web archives) to discover known subdomains without active scanning.

Active

Linked & JS Discovery

GoSpider / Hakrawler: Spider the target site recursively, extracting all linked URLs and JavaScript file references to find subdomains referenced in client-side code.

Active

Subdomain Brute-Force

Amass / ffuf: Attempt to resolve domains constructed from common prefixes (mail, vpn, dev, staging, api) against the target's DNS to find unlisted subdomains.

Passive

JS Entropy Analysis

SubDomainizer: Analyzes JavaScript files for subdomains, cloud service references, and Shannon entropy anomalies that may indicate embedded API keys or tokens.

Shodan: Discovering Exposed Infrastructure

Shodan is a continuous internet-wide scanner that captures service banners, certificate data, and stack profiling information from every reachable IP address. For a penetration tester, Shodan is a passive window into what an organization's infrastructure looks like from the outside — without probing it directly.

Shodan searches can surface: exposed remote desktop services, industrial control systems (ICS/SCADA), unpatched software versions, default login pages, and misconfigured cloud storage buckets — all without a single packet reaching the target.

Phase 3 — Infrastructure Analysis

Port Scanning: Masscan + Nmap Chain

Port scanning is the first active technique in the methodology — it sends packets to the target and is detectable. For this reason, it is always conducted within explicit written scope. The standard approach chains two tools for efficiency and accuracy:

masscan -p1-65535 -iL $ipFile --max-rate 1800 -oG $output.log # Feed masscan results to nmap for service fingerprinting nmap -sV -iL masscan_results.txt -oG nmap_services.log # Run Brutespray against remote admin protocols for default credentials brutespray --file nmap_services.log

Masscan achieves its speed through a custom TCP/IP stack implementation and true multi-threading. The tradeoff is accuracy — masscan is used for rapid port discovery, then nmap performs slower, more accurate service version detection on the discovered open ports.

Screenshot Enumeration

At scale — hundreds or thousands of discovered web-facing services — it is not feasible to manually browse each one. Screenshot tools (Eyewitness, Aquatone) automatically capture screenshots of every discovered web service, enabling rapid visual triage to identify high-value targets: login portals, admin interfaces, default-configured applications, and accidentally exposed development environments.

Phase 4 — Automation & Scale

Enterprise-grade penetration tests involve attack surfaces with thousands of assets. Manual execution of reconnaissance steps becomes the bottleneck. Automation frameworks address this by chaining tools, managing input/output formats, adding threading, and enabling distributed execution across multiple machines.

TierCharacteristicsCapability Level
C-Tier Bash/Python scripts chaining existing tools. Step-based, no workflow management. Few techniques. Entry-level automation
B-Tier Custom modules for some functions. Basic GUI or workflow. Runs point-in-time. Flat file output. Intermediate
A-Tier All custom modules. GUI-driven. Runs iteratively. Manages data via database. Scales. Professional
S-Tier Custom modules + GUI + iterative execution + database + multi-box scaling + alerts + ML/AI + novel techniques. Elite red team

A practical entry point for automation is Interlace, a framework that adds threading, CIDR/glob input support, proxying, and command queuing to existing tools — without requiring their rewriting. Combined with tools from the tomnomnom ecosystem (httprobe, waybackurls, anew), it enables scalable recon pipelines buildable in hours rather than weeks.

Phase 5 — Internal Situational Awareness

If reconnaissance leads to a foothold inside the network, the methodology transitions to internal reconnaissance — understanding the Active Directory (AD) environment, which underpins Windows networks in virtually all enterprise environments.

Active Directory stores every network object — servers, workstations, users, printers, applications — as a queryable object with attributes. Understanding AD structure reveals: privileged accounts, trust relationships between domains, reachable network segments, and the organizational hierarchy of access. Internal recon is a precursor to privilege escalation, lateral movement, and ultimately, domain compromise.

For Defenders
Understanding how attackers enumerate your AD environment is essential for hardening it. Enable AD audit logging (Event IDs 4624, 4625, 4648, 4768, 4769, 4776). Monitor for LDAP queries from unexpected sources. Implement tiered administration to limit the blast radius of credential compromise at any single tier.

What Organizations Can Do to Reduce Their Reconnaissance Surface

Google Yourself First

Conduct regular passive reconnaissance against your own organization. Search for leaked credentials on GitHub, check your Shodan exposure, enumerate your own subdomains with Subfinder. Know what an attacker would find before they do.

Scan Repos for Secrets

Deploy tools like Gitleaks or TruffleHog in your CI/CD pipeline to detect credential patterns before code is committed. Retroactively scan existing repositories. Treat a leaked secret as a confirmed compromise — rotate it immediately.

Maintain an Asset Inventory

You cannot protect what you do not know you have. Implement continuous asset discovery — not a quarterly spreadsheet. Shadow IT (assets deployed without IT knowledge) are disproportionately likely to be unpatched and misconfigured.

Reduce OSINT Footprint

Audit job postings for unnecessary technology disclosure. Configure WHOIS privacy on domain registrations. Remove excess DNS records (unused subdomains, development domains). Reduce the intelligence gift you leave for adversaries.

Close Unnecessary Ports & Services

Shodan your own IP ranges. Any service visible from the internet that is not intended to be public is a finding. SSH and RDP should never be directly internet-accessible; use jump hosts, VPNs, or Zero Trust Network Access instead.

Monitor for Recon Signatures

Anomalous DNS query volumes, rapid sequential port scans, and unusual subdomain resolution patterns are observable signals of active reconnaissance. Implement honeypots and canary tokens in DNS to detect passive enumeration attempts.

Key Takeaways

  • Reconnaissance is not hacking — yet. The passive phase of reconnaissance uses only public information. The most damaging intelligence an attacker gathers may come entirely from sources your organization published itself: job boards, GitHub, LinkedIn, your own website.
  • Forgotten assets are the highest-risk assets. Subdomains, legacy applications, and cloud resources that an organization has stopped actively maintaining are disproportionately likely to be unpatched, misconfigured, and unmonitored.
  • The attack surface is larger than most organizations realize. A single enterprise domain may yield hundreds of subdomains and dozens of IP ranges through systematic enumeration. Every one of those represents a potential entry point.
  • Understanding offensive techniques is essential for defensive design. Security teams that understand how attackers enumerate their environment make better decisions about what to monitor, what to expose, and where to invest in hardening.
  • Automation amplifies capability — on both sides. Attackers use automation to enumerate attack surfaces at scale. Defenders must use the same tools to understand their own exposure continuously, not once a year.
