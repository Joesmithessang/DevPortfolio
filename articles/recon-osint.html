How Ethical Hackers Do Their Homework Before an Attack

A behind-the-scenes look at “reconnaissance” — how authorized security testers quietly map out everything an organization exposes to the internet before testing its defenses — and, just as importantly, how you can shrink what they (and real attackers) find.

Joe Smith-Essang Discipline: Penetration Testing / Red Team Methodology, 2021 12 min read
In Plain Terms
Penetration testing is hiring authorized experts (“ethical hackers”) to attack your systems the way a criminal would, so you can fix the weaknesses first. Reconnaissance is the homework they do before that: quietly gathering information about the target. A lot of it is OSINT — “open-source intelligence,” which just means information that's already public, like your website, staff LinkedIn profiles, and job ads. This article shows what that homework reveals — and how to give away less of it.

Why the Homework Phase Matters Most

In professional security testing, reconnaissance isn't a warm-up — it's the foundation everything else is built on. The better the information gathered up front, the sharper and more effective the test. Rushed homework produces a shallow test; thorough homework uncovers the unexpected ways in that no automated tool would ever spot.

This article is based on a reconnaissance approach refined across many authorized tests of large organizations. None of this is a how-to for unauthorized use. Every technique here is used only within an authorized test, governed by a signed agreement (a “Letter of Engagement”) that sets the legal boundaries before anything begins.

Authorization First
Every technique described in this article requires explicit written authorization from the target organization. A Letter of Engagement defining scope, testing window, emergency contacts, and authorized activities is a non-negotiable prerequisite. Testing without authorization is illegal and unethical — full stop.

A security test runs in stages. Reconnaissance comes early, before any actual break-in attempt, and everything it uncovers feeds the stages that follow.

Testing Blind, on Purpose

The approach here assumes a “blind” test: the tester starts like a real outside attacker, knowing nothing about the target's inner workings. The client simply says “everything you can reach from the internet is fair game” — which means the tester first has to discover what that includes. That's exactly why good reconnaissance matters so much.

The Unsung Hero: Good Note-Taking

Before running any tool, you need a solid way to organize what you find. Reconnaissance produces a mountain of information — web addresses, staff names, technologies in use, exposed passwords, and more. Being able to find and connect that information later is what makes the final report valuable. There's no single right tool; what matters is being consistent. Well-organized findings are useful; a messy pile is just noise.

Step 1 — Gathering Public Information (Without Touching Anything)

This first step uses only information that's already public — without ever contacting the target's systems. It's completely legal, and it routinely surprises organizations with just how much they've unknowingly put on display.

Your Website, LinkedIn & Job Ads

An organization's own online presence is a goldmine. From the website, staff social media, and job adverts, a skilled analyst can piece together:

  • The email format — knowing it's, say, firstname.lastname@company.com lets an attacker guess staff email addresses for targeted scam emails
  • Which technology you use — technical job ads often name the exact security products in use, revealing what defenses an attacker will face
  • Key people — the names and roles of IT and security staff are useful for impersonation scams and for understanding who does what
  • Partners and suppliers — mentions of outside providers point to possible indirect ways in
Defender Insight
Review your own organization's job postings from an attacker's perspective. Ask: does this listing reveal which security tools we use? Does it disclose our cloud provider? Technical specificity in job postings is a competitive necessity but also an intelligence gift to adversaries. Consider how much technology detail is truly necessary.

Advanced Google Searches (“Google Dorking”)

Google has advanced search tricks that go far beyond a normal search. Using them, a tester can dig up documents, login pages, and forgotten websites an organization didn't realize were public. Searching for the exact wording of a company's copyright or privacy notice, for example, can surface related sites it has stopped maintaining. The examples below show the kind of searches used:

site:target.com ext:pdf inurl:"login" site:target.com "copyright 2024 TargetOrg" -site:target.com intitle:"index of" site:target.com

There's even a public catalogue of these search tricks aimed at finding specific weaknesses — exposed admin pages, configuration files, and the like.

Searching Public Code for Leaked Secrets

As development teams grow, sooner or later someone accidentally posts sensitive material to a public code-sharing site. Testers search these sites for the organization's name and products to find secrets left in the open by mistake:

org:TargetOrg "password" extension:config org:TargetOrg "api_key" language:python "target.com" "secret" pushed:>2023-01-01
A Very Common Finding
In a large share of these tests, at least one developer has left a working password or access key in public code — often keys to databases or internal systems that were meant to stay private. These are especially dangerous because there's no “hacking” involved: the attacker just reads the exposed secret and walks in.

Step 2 — Mapping What's Connected to the Internet

Next, the tester turns that public information into a map of everything the organization has facing the internet: its websites, servers, and cloud services — including the ones the organization has forgotten it even owns. Those forgotten systems are often the most vulnerable.

Finding the Organization's Internet Addresses

Large organizations are assigned blocks of internet addresses. By looking these up in public registries, a tester can uncover the full range of systems an organization runs — including old, neglected ones. One catch: systems hosted in the cloud (like Amazon or Microsoft) don't show up this way, so testers also search public certificate records, which reveal website names no matter where they're hosted.

Finding Related Websites

Public domain-registration records can be searched in reverse: starting from a company's name or contact email, you can list all the web addresses registered to it. This often turns up related sites the organization never advertises — acquired brands, test sites, and old domains it forgot to shut down.

Finding Hidden Sub-Sites

A single web address like company.com can hide hundreds of sub-sites (such as mail.company.com or vpn.company.com), each a separate service — and many are looked after far less carefully than the main website. Testers find these in a few complementary ways:

Quiet

Checking Public Records

Gather sub-site names from public sources like certificate logs and web archives — without ever contacting the target.

Active

Following the Links

Automatically crawl the website, following every link and reference to discover other sites it connects to.

Active

Educated Guessing

Test common names (mail, vpn, dev, staging, api) to see which ones exist — turning up sub-sites that aren't listed anywhere.

Quiet

Reading the Website's Code

Scan the site's own behind-the-scenes code, which often mentions other sub-sites and cloud services — and sometimes even leaked keys.

Using Shodan, the “Search Engine for Devices”

Shodan is a service that continuously scans the whole internet and records what it finds on every reachable device. For a tester, it's a way to see what an organization looks like from the outside — without probing it directly.

A Shodan search can reveal exposed remote-access services, industrial control systems, out-of-date software, default login pages, and misconfigured cloud storage — all without ever touching the target's systems.

Step 3 — Probing the Systems Found

Checking Which Doors Are Open (Port Scanning)

This is the first step that actually contacts the target's systems, so it can be detected — which is why it's only ever done within the agreed, written scope. The tester checks which network “doors” (ports) are open and what's running behind them. In practice, a fast tool finds the open doors, then a more careful tool identifies exactly what each one is:

masscan -p1-65535 -iL $ipFile --max-rate 1800 -oG $output.log # Then identify what's running on each open port nmap -sV -iL masscan_results.txt -oG nmap_services.log

The fast tool trades detail for speed; the careful tool then fills in the detail on just the open doors it found. Together they're both quick and accurate.

Taking Screenshots at Scale

When you've found hundreds or thousands of web pages, you can't open them all by hand. Tools automatically take a screenshot of each one, so the tester can quickly eyeball them and spot the interesting targets: login pages, admin panels, and forgotten test sites left exposed.

Step 4 — Doing It All at Scale

A big organization can have thousands of systems, and doing every step by hand becomes the bottleneck. So testers automate: stringing tools together, feeding the output of one into the next, and running many checks at once. As a rough guide, automation maturity climbs through four levels:

LevelWhat it looks likeWho's here
Basic Simple scripts stringing existing tools together, run step by step by hand. Beginners
Intermediate Some custom-built pieces and a basic interface; run when needed. Improving
Advanced Mostly custom-built, runs itself repeatedly, and stores results in an organized database. Professionals
Elite Fully custom, runs continuously across many machines, sends alerts, and uses AI to work smarter. Top red teams

You don't need to build the elite version to benefit. Free, off-the-shelf tools let a small team stitch together a capable, automated reconnaissance process in a matter of hours rather than weeks.

Step 5 — Looking Around From Inside

If a test succeeds in getting a foothold inside the network, the focus shifts to understanding the inside. In most Windows-based organizations, that means studying the central directory system (Active Directory) — the master list that keeps track of every user, computer, and printer, and who's allowed to do what.

Understanding this central directory shows an attacker where the powerful accounts are, which systems they can reach, and how access is organized — the map they need to move deeper and, ultimately, take over the whole network.

For Defenders
Because attackers rely on this map, watch it closely. Keep detailed logs of logins and directory look-ups, watch for unusual queries coming from unexpected places, and organize your powerful accounts into tiers — so that compromising one account doesn't hand over everything at once.

How to Give Attackers Less to Find

Investigate Yourself First

Do this same homework on your own organization regularly. Search public code sites for your leaked passwords, check what Shodan shows about you, and list your own sub-sites. Know what an attacker would find before they do.

Scan Your Code for Secrets

Use automated tools to catch passwords and keys before they get saved into code, and scan your existing code too. If a secret does leak, treat it as compromised and change it immediately.

Keep a Live Inventory of What You Own

You can't protect what you don't know you have. Keep a continuously updated list of your systems, not a spreadsheet updated once a quarter. Systems set up without IT's knowledge are the ones most likely to be neglected and vulnerable.

Share Less in Public

Trim unnecessary technical detail from job ads, turn on privacy for your domain registrations, and shut down old sub-sites and test sites you no longer use. Every detail you remove is one less clue for an attacker.

Close Doors You Don't Need Open

Check what of yours is visible on the internet. Anything exposed that doesn't need to be is a problem. In particular, remote-admin access should never be open directly to the internet — put it behind a secure gateway.

Watch for Signs of Snooping

Bursts of unusual look-ups or rapid scanning of your systems can signal an attacker doing their homework. You can even plant harmless “tripwires” that quietly alert you the moment someone pokes at them.

Key Takeaways

  • Most of this isn't hacking — it's reading. The bulk of what an attacker learns comes from information you published yourself: job ads, code sites, LinkedIn, your own website.
  • Forgotten systems are the riskiest. The sub-sites, old apps, and cloud services no one maintains anymore are exactly the ones most likely to be out of date and unwatched.
  • You're probably more exposed than you think. A single company web address can hide hundreds of sub-sites. Each one is a possible way in.
  • Knowing the attacker's playbook helps you defend. Understanding how attackers do their homework tells you what to hide, what to watch, and where to invest.
  • Do this to yourself, regularly. Attackers scan for exposure constantly. The best defense is to check your own exposure just as often — not once a year.
