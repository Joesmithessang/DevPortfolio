Eyes Below the Surface:
Dark Web Intelligence for Business Security

Stolen passwords and data often end up for sale in the hidden corners of the internet — and that directly affects your organization. A plain-language guide to what the dark web actually is, how to watch it for signs your business has been exposed, and what to do when it has.

March 2026 8 min read Joe Smith-Essang Threat Intelligence & CTI

Context

This article is based on a knowledge-sharing session delivered to a corporate security team. It captures the practical, hands-on approach used to set up dark web monitoring during a consulting engagement. All client-specific details have been removed.

In Plain Terms

The dark web is a part of the internet you can't reach with a normal browser or find on Google. It's deliberately hidden, which makes it a marketplace where criminals buy and sell stolen passwords, customer data, and access to company networks. Dark web monitoring simply means keeping watch on these places so you find out your organization's data is being traded before criminals use it against you.

The Three Layers of the Internet

Most people only ever use a thin slice of the internet. Picturing it as three layers makes it much easier to understand where your organization is exposed and why.

Surface Web

~4% of internet content — publicly indexed by search engines

Everything you can reach with a normal browser and find on Google. This is where your company website, news sites, and social media live. Surprisingly, it's also where some criminals openly brag about breaches and post samples of stolen data to pressure victims.

google.comLinkedInNews sites GitHubPublic forums

Deep Web

~96% of internet content — not on search engines, needs a login

The vast majority of the internet. There's nothing sinister about it — it's simply everything that sits behind a login, like your email, online banking, or company intranet. The danger is that a stolen password sold on the dark web is the key that unlocks these private areas. That's why watching for leaked passwords is so important.

Online banking portalsCorporate intranets Medical recordsEmail accountsCloud storage

Dark Web

Deliberately hidden — needs special anonymising software to reach

Content that is deliberately hidden and reachable only with special anonymising software (the best known is the Tor browser), which hides who is visiting. This is where criminals run leak sites to publish stolen data, sell passwords, and even sell ready-made access into companies. If your organization's data shows up here, it usually means an attack is already underway or a leak is being threatened.

What Ends Up for Sale

Criminals buy and sell many kinds of stolen information. Knowing what's traded helps you decide what's most important to watch for.

Employee Credentials

Harvested via phishing, stealer malware, or breaches of third-party SaaS providers.

Financial Data

Customer card data, bank account credentials, and transaction records from breached systems.

Personal & Health Records

Patient records, national ID numbers, and biometric data — extremely valuable for fraud and identity theft.

Ways Into Your Network

Working remote-access logins to company systems, sold ready-made by specialists who break in and then resell the access.

Hacking Tools

Ready-made attack software and newly discovered weaknesses, sold to anyone willing to pay — lowering the skill needed to attack.

Proprietary Data

Stolen source code, R&D documents, contracts, and M&A information used for extortion or competitive espionage.

How Dark Web Monitoring Works: Five Steps

Good monitoring isn't just leaving a tool running in the background. It's a repeatable process, and it helps to think of it in five simple steps — from deciding what to watch, through spotting and checking a warning sign, to acting on it and telling the right people.

01

Preparation

Decide exactly what you want to protect, and set up a safe way to watch for it. This step shapes everything that follows.

  • List what matters most: company email domains, executive names, product names, and sensitive project names
  • Turn each of those into search terms to watch for
  • Set up separate, isolated computers to do the searching safely (so the monitoring can't be traced back or put your own network at risk)
  • Decide who does what: who watches, who double-checks, and who coordinates the response
  • Agree a secure way to raise alerts that doesn't rely on company email — in case that email has itself been compromised
02

Detection

Automated systems scan dark web forums, paste sites, breach databases, ransomware leak portals, and Telegram channels for keywords matching your monitored assets.

  • Automated crawlers index .onion sites, clearnet hacker forums, and paste services continuously
  • Alert triggers on exact-match credentials (email domains), IP mentions, data dumps containing monitored patterns
  • Ransomware leak site monitoring for organization name or industry sector listings
  • Telegram & Discord channel monitoring for threat actor communications referencing targets
03

Analysis & Verification

A raw alert on its own can be misleading. Before reacting, you need to check whether it's real and whether it actually relates to you. Five questions to ask about every alert:

  • Source: What forum/channel/site? What is its historical reliability and threat actor affiliation?
  • Author Profile: Is the poster a known threat actor, first-time poster, or likely reseller? Check account history and reputation score.
  • Activity Pattern: Is this part of a coordinated campaign or an isolated post? Cross-reference with known threat actor activity timelines.
  • Content Context: Is the data fresh or recycled from an older breach? Does it include internal indicators (hostnames, internal IPs) that confirm it is genuinely from your environment?
  • Verification: Confirm validity of credentials without activating them (e.g., check if email address exists without attempting login). If it is an access listing, verify the IP/VPN endpoint matches your infrastructure.
04

Response

Once a finding is confirmed, act in proportion to how serious it is — a live, working password needs a faster, bigger response than an old one from a years-old breach. The full response playbook is in the next section.

  • Severity triage: active credential exposure vs. stale data vs. reconnaissance mention
  • Immediate credential lock for confirmed active exposures
  • Forensic investigation to determine initial breach source
  • Eliminate the breach vector before re-issuing credentials
  • Notify affected parties per applicable data protection regulations
05

Communication

A finding is only useful if it reaches the right people in a form they can act on. Different audiences need different levels of detail.

  • Senior leaders: a short summary — what the business impact is, how serious it is, and what's being done about it
  • Security team: the full technical detail, including where it was found and how to detect it in future
  • Legal/Compliance: whether the law requires you to notify anyone, plus the evidence to support it
  • Affected individuals: notification letters where personal data is confirmed exposed
  • Feed findings back into the Preparation phase to update monitoring keywords and improve future detection

What to Do When an Account Is Exposed

A leaked password is by far the most common thing you'll find. Here's a simple five-stage plan for what to do once you've confirmed one of your accounts has been exposed — lock it, reset it, investigate, remove the attacker's foothold, then restore and report.

ACCOUNT COMPROMISE — RESPONSE PLAYBOOK

STAGE 01

Lock

  • Immediately disable the compromised account(s)
  • Revoke all active sessions and tokens
  • Block MFA recovery options
  • Alert the account owner

STAGE 02

Reset

  • Force password reset via out-of-band channel
  • Re-enroll MFA device
  • Review & revoke API keys and OAuth grants
  • Check for forwarding rules / mail exfil

STAGE 03

Investigate

  • Audit login history for suspicious geolocations
  • Review email sent items for data exfiltration
  • Check file access & downloads
  • Determine how credentials were obtained

STAGE 04

Eliminate

  • Remove attacker-planted persistence (rules, delegates)
  • Remediate the breach vector (phishing, stealer malware)
  • Scan endpoint for stealer malware
  • Block attacker IPs at perimeter

STAGE 05

Recover & Report

  • Restore access to the verified user
  • Complete incident documentation
  • Notify regulator if personal data affected
  • Update monitoring keywords & detection rules

Judging Whether an Alert Is Real

Not every alert is genuine or relevant. Weighing it against these five points helps you separate a real threat from background noise or an old, recycled leak:

Source Credibility

Known forum, threat actor group affiliation, historical posting accuracy, and reputation score within the community.

Author Profiling

Account age, posting history, claimed skills, prior listings sold, known aliases, and community trust level.

Pattern of Activity

Is this a one-off, or part of a wider campaign? Are other organizations in your industry being targeted at the same time? Does the timing line up with a newly announced software weakness?

Content Context

Sample data provided? Does it contain internal indicators (hostnames, private IPs, internal terminology) confirming it is genuinely from your environment?

Double-Check It

Compare against your own system logs, check known breach databases (such as Have I Been Pwned), and confirm the data really is yours before raising the alarm.

Why It's Worth the Investment

Organizations that monitor the dark web gain three benefits that build on each other:

Threat Intelligence & Threat Hunting

Early detection of your organization's exposure — whether through credentials, sensitive documents, or network access listings — provides the intelligence needed to proactively hunt threats before they become incidents.

Faster Incident Response

Dark web signals often precede a full breach by days or weeks. Early warning enables a contained, faster response compared to discovering the breach after the attacker has already operated in your environment.

Safeguard Organizational Interests

Protecting trade secrets, customers' personal data, and executives' accounts reduces reputational harm, regulatory fines, and business disruption. Monitoring costs a small fraction of what a breach costs.

Where This Is Heading

Dark web monitoring is changing fast, as both attackers and defenders get better tools. A few trends worth knowing:

Artificial Intelligence Doing the First Pass

AI is starting to handle the initial sorting and checking of alerts, freeing up human analysts. It can also spot indirect or disguised references to your organization — not just exact keyword matches — catching mentions that simple searches would miss.

Faster, More Automated Response

Tools now watch thousands of sources at once and can kick off an automatic response — such as locking an exposed account — in minutes rather than days.

Organizations Sharing Warnings

Industry groups are formalizing how companies share threat information. When one bank is attacked, the details become an early warning for every other bank in the group — so an attack on one becomes a defense for all.

Growing Legal Pressure

Data protection laws increasingly demand that breaches be reported within tight deadlines — deadlines that are hard to meet without monitoring already in place. In several countries it's shifting from “good practice” to a legal expectation.

A Constant Cat-and-Mouse on Hiding

As criminals adopt newer ways to stay hidden, monitoring tools keep adapting to keep up. This is why it usually makes sense to work with a specialist provider whose whole job is keeping pace — rather than trying to do it all in-house.

Recommendations for Your Organization

Use a Monitoring Service

Specialist services watch the dark web around the clock and have experts review what they find. It's worth the cost even for mid-size organizations — nearly every company has some passwords exposed somewhere.

Focus on What Matters Most

Don't try to watch everything. Decide which things would hurt most if exposed — company email addresses, senior executives' accounts, key product and project names — and concentrate your monitoring there.

Agree Your Response in Advance

Spotting a problem is no use if nobody knows what to do next. Decide ahead of time who gets told, when you force a password reset, and when to involve legal — not in the heat of the moment.

Help Staff Handle Passwords Safely

Most leaked passwords come from phishing, or from staff reusing their work password on a personal site that later gets breached. Providing a password manager and running phishing practice drills cuts this risk sharply.

Turn On a Second Login Step Everywhere

A stolen password is nearly useless if a second login step (MFA) is required. It's the single most effective defense against stolen passwords, no matter how they were obtained.

Review the Outside Services You Use

Staff passwords often leak through breaches of the third-party online tools they sign up for. Keep track of which outside services use your work email addresses, and close any that are unused or unnecessary.

Key Takeaways

  • This is real, not theoretical. Your organization's passwords and data are genuinely bought and sold in these places. Monitoring is a practical necessity, not a nice-to-have.
  • An alert is only a starting point. Always check whether it's genuine and relevant before reacting — otherwise you'll drown in false alarms.
  • Leaks usually come before the breach. Seeing your data appear on the dark web is an early warning that lets you act before an attacker does.
  • A second login step and good password habits are the cheapest, most effective ways to blunt the impact of a leaked password.
  • Feed what you learn back in. Every finding should sharpen what you watch for and what you teach your staff.
