Ransomware in the Enterprise:
A Consultant's Field Account

A real ransomware attack on a large insurance group, explained in plain language — how the attack unfolded, how we stopped it, how the organization recovered without paying, and what any business can do to be better prepared.

February 2023 9 min read Joe Smith-Essang Threat Response & Forensics

Client Confidentiality Notice

All client-identifying information has been anonymized. The technical methodology, attack indicators, and response playbook are drawn from direct engagement experience and are shared here purely for educational purposes.

In Plain Terms

Ransomware is malicious software that locks up an organization's files and demands payment to unlock them. In a double-extortion attack, the criminals also steal a copy of the data first, then threaten to publish it — so paying to unlock the files still doesn't stop the leak. In this case, a large insurance company had every computer locked. Because it kept a set of backups completely disconnected from its network, it was able to rebuild everything without paying the criminals a cent.

What Happened

A large regional insurance group was hit by a well-known criminal group called BlackCat (also known as ALPHV). The attackers locked every computer on the company's network and, at the same time, stole sensitive customer data to use as extra leverage. Even the company's online backups — the copies it would normally rely on to recover — were locked, because those backups were connected to the same network.

I was brought in to lead the response: stop the attack from spreading, work out who was behind it, preserve the evidence, and guide the recovery in line with the rules that apply to financial companies. This article turns that experience into a clear, step-by-step approach and a set of practical recommendations any organization can use to prepare before something like this happens.

How the Attack Unfolded

A common myth is that ransomware locks everything the instant it arrives. In reality, attackers usually spend days quietly moving around inside a network first. By piecing together the evidence afterwards, we could see the attackers had been inside for about a week before they triggered the lock-up. Here is that week, step by step.

Day 1 — Getting in

The attackers slip inside

The criminals gained their first foothold, most likely by tricking an employee with a fake email (phishing) or by logging in through an internet-facing system using a stolen password.

Days 3–5 — Looking around

Mapping the network

Rather than bringing their own noisy tools, the attackers used the everyday administration tools already built into the company's own systems. This let them quietly map the network and find the most valuable systems — including where the backups lived — without setting off alarms.

Days 6–7 — Preparing the blow

Stealing the keys and the data

The attackers captured the master administrator passwords, which act like a master key to the whole network. They used that access to delete the online backups and quietly copy out sensitive customer data to their own servers.

Day 8 — The lock-up

Every system locked at once

Using the master access they had stolen, the attackers pushed the ransomware to every reachable computer simultaneously. A ransom note appeared in every folder, and the business ground to a complete halt.

Hour 0 — We step in

Response begins

Our team was engaged and immediately began working out how far the attack had spread, cutting off the affected areas, and notifying the national cyber authority (CERT) for support.

Hour 4 — Under control

The attack is contained

Within four hours, every affected part of the network was cut off so the attack could spread no further. We recorded the attackers' fingerprints (see below), safely captured evidence, and began planning recovery from the offline backups.

Who Was Behind It

Working out who is responsible matters, because it tells you how they operate and what they are likely to do next. The evidence pointed to BlackCat, a professional criminal group that rents out its ransomware to other criminals for a cut of the profits — a business model the industry calls Ransomware-as-a-Service. How We Responded, Step by Step

We worked through six stages, in order. Two goals mattered equally: stopping the attack quickly, and carefully preserving the evidence so we could understand what happened and meet legal requirements. The grey boxes show the specific technical actions for readers who work in security; everyone else can safely skip them — the plain-language explanation is in the paragraph above each.

01

Immediate Network Isolation

The first and most time-critical action is severing the adversary's active access. All affected network segments were immediately isolated at the switch level to prevent further lateral movement. Internet egress from infected segments was blocked at the firewall. Business-critical systems on unaffected segments remained operational under enhanced monitoring.

ACTION: Block inter-VLAN routing from infected segments → core switch ACLs
ACTION: Disable affected AD accounts & revoke Kerberos tickets (klist purge)
ACTION: Kill VPN sessions from all remote endpoints pending assessment
02

Forensic Snapshot Preservation

Critical lesson: before any recovery operation begins, forensic-grade snapshots of all affected VMs must be taken at the hypervisor level. This is non-negotiable. Recovery wipes the forensic artefacts needed to reconstruct the attack timeline, attribute the actor, and support regulatory investigations.

ACTION: Create VM snapshots on ESXi/Hyper-V — do NOT power off without snapshot
ACTION: Export raw disk images (.vmdk / .vhd) to isolated forensic storage
ACTION: Document chain of custody for all forensic artefacts
03

Threat Actor Attribution

Attribution was achieved by analysing the dropped ransom note, correlating file extension patterns, and cross-referencing IOCs against threat intelligence repositories. BlackCat's Rust-based payload and its characteristic “RECOVER-<extension>-FILES.txt” naming convention confirmed the actor. IOC packages were then shared with the national CERT and sectoral regulatory body.

TOOL: OSForensics — hash extraction & malware artefact recovery
TOOL: Volatility — memory forensics to recover in-memory payload artifacts
TOOL: RegRipper — registry timeline analysis for persistence mechanisms
04

Multi-Layer IOC Blocking

Extracted indicators of compromise (file hashes, IP addresses, domain names, ransom note filenames) were pushed to every available control layer to prevent re-infection and cut any remaining C2 channels.

LAYER 1 (Endpoint): Hash blacklisting via EDR policy push
LAYER 2 (Email Gateway): Domain/sender reputation rules updated
LAYER 3 (DNS): Sinkholing C2 domains at internal resolver
LAYER 4 (Firewall/Proxy): IP & URL blocklist enrichment
LAYER 5 (SIEM): Detection rules for re-appearance of IOC pattern
05

Supervised Credential Reset & Access Rebuild

Because domain credentials were confirmed compromised, a supervised enterprise-wide credential reset was mandatory before any recovery could begin. All privileged accounts were reset first, followed by standard user accounts. MFA was enforced on all remote access paths before any system was reconnected to the network.

PRIORITY ORDER: Domain Admin → Service Accounts → Standard Users
ACTION: Force password reset at next logon via AD policy
ACTION: Enroll all admin accounts into hardware MFA (FIDO2 tokens)
ACTION: Audit service account SPNs for Kerberoastable accounts
06

Offline Backup Restoration & Post-Incident Reporting

Recovery was achieved exclusively from offline (air-gapped) backups — the online backup infrastructure had been encrypted along with the rest of the estate. Systems were rebuilt in order of business criticality. Post-incident, a formal maturity assessment was conducted to identify the gaps that allowed the attack to succeed, and a regulatory notification package was prepared per applicable sectoral requirements.

OUTCOME: Full recovery without ransom payment
TOOL: CrowdStrike / Cortex XDR EDR deployed on all recovered endpoints before reconnection
DELIVERABLE: Incident timeline report + regulatory notification package

The Four Biggest Lessons

1. A backup the attacker can reach is a backup you can lose

Many organizations assume that having backups means they are safe. But if a backup is connected to the network, an attacker who takes over the network can lock or delete it too — which is exactly what happened here. The attackers went after the backups first. The only copies that survived were the ones kept completely offline and disconnected. If you can reach your backup from your everyday network, so can an attacker.

Critical Finding

Online backups were encrypted alongside the production environment, eliminating that recovery path entirely. Offline, air-gapped backups were the only mechanism that enabled recovery without paying the ransom.

2. Save the evidence before you start cleaning up

When systems are down, everyone wants to wipe the infected machines and get back to work as fast as possible. But wiping a machine also destroys the evidence of how the attack happened. We insisted on taking exact copies (snapshots) of the affected systems before touching anything. Those copies were what let us reconstruct the timeline, identify the attackers, and produce the report regulators required. Rushing to rebuild would have thrown all of that away.

3. The master administrator accounts are the real target

The attackers were only able to lock everything at once because they had stolen the master administrator passwords — the accounts that control the entire network. The fact that they reached that level of access so quickly pointed to weak password practices and a lack of extra protection around those powerful accounts. Protecting these “keys to the kingdom” is one of the most valuable things any organization can do.

4. Call the national cyber authority early — don't hide the incident

Notifying the national cyber authority (CERT) early paid off: they provided extra intelligence, helped confirm who was behind the attack, and coordinated with the regulator. Many organizations delay this call because they worry about their reputation. That is a mistake — getting help early shortens the whole recovery and often improves how the incident is judged later.

Recommendations for Your Organization

These are not theoretical controls. They are the specific gaps that this engagement exposed, formulated as actionable recommendations for any organization seeking to reduce its ransomware risk.

Implement Offline & Immutable Backups

Follow the 3-2-1-1-0 rule: 3 copies, 2 media types, 1 off-site, 1 offline (air-gapped), 0 errors (tested). Test restoration quarterly — untested backups are not backups.

Segment Your Network

Micro-segment OT, backup infrastructure, and high-value asset zones from the general corporate network. Lateral movement should require explicit firewall policy, not default trust.

Require a Second Login Step (MFA)

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) means a password alone isn't enough — a second check, like a code or a physical security key, is also needed. Require it for all remote access. For the powerful admin accounts, use physical security keys, which stop attackers even if they steal the password.

Lock Down Your Most Powerful Accounts

Don't leave master-administrator access switched on all the time. Grant it only when needed and for a limited time, keep the passwords in a secured vault, and record what those accounts do. Tools for this are called Privileged Access Management (PAM).

Put Modern Protection on Every Device

Traditional antivirus looks for known bad files and misses modern ransomware. Newer tools (called EDR) watch for suspicious behaviour instead. Every device must be covered — a single unprotected machine is a way in.

Test Your Ransomware IR Playbook

A playbook on paper is not a playbook. Conduct tabletop exercises and purple team simulations specifically for ransomware scenarios to validate your response capability before the real event.

Get Early Warnings About Active Threats

Threat intelligence feeds share up-to-date information about criminal groups and their current attacks. Knowing which groups are targeting your industry — and how — lets you block them before they reach you.

Watch for Backups Being Deleted

Attackers usually delete backups just before locking everything. Set up an alert for any unexpected backup deletion — it's an early warning sign that gives you a chance to act before the damage is done.

Key Takeaways

  • Speed of containment is what you control. Once the lock-up starts, cutting off the affected areas within the first hour is the single biggest thing that limits the damage.
  • Preserve evidence before cleaning up. Without it, you can't understand the attack, identify who did it, or satisfy regulators — and rebuilding destroys it forever.
  • Offline backups are your safety net. A set of backups kept fully disconnected from the network was the one thing that made recovery possible without paying.
  • Protect your most powerful accounts. Strong passwords, a second login step, and tight control over master-admin accounts are the best-value investments you can make before an attack.
  • Build relationships before a crisis. Knowing your national cyber authority and regulator ahead of time means faster help when it counts. Make those connections now, not on the worst day.

Outcome

The organization achieved full recovery from offline backups with zero ransom payment. Post-incident, a comprehensive security maturity assessment was delivered, a formal EDR deployment programme was initiated, and backup architecture was overhauled to an air-gapped, immutable design. A regulatory notification was filed within the required timeframe.
