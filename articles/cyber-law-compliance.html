Cybercrime Law and Working Together: What the Rules Require

A plain-language tour of the laws that make cybersecurity a legal duty for financial firms — what they demand, what happens if you ignore them, and why sharing threat information with peers is one of the most powerful and underused defenses available.

Joe Smith-Essang Scope: Nigeria, West Africa, Global Advisory & Training, 2025 11 min read
In Plain Terms
Cybersecurity isn't just good practice — for financial firms, a lot of it is required by law. If you get it wrong, the consequences go beyond a breach: regulators can fine you, directors can be held personally responsible (even face prison), and customers can sue. This article explains, in everyday language, the main laws involved and why teaming up with other organizations to share warnings is such an effective defense.

Why the Law Now Requires Good Security

For financial firms, cybersecurity is a legal duty, not just a technical one. When a breach happens, the fallout goes well beyond the disruption itself: fines from regulators, personal liability for the executives in charge, and lawsuits from affected customers. Knowing what the law expects is now a basic part of the job.

This article draws on cybercrime-law training and intelligence-sharing workshops delivered to financial organizations in West Africa and beyond. It covers the main laws you need to know, the real-world reasons cybercriminals are so hard to prosecute, and why sharing threat information with other organizations makes everyone stronger.

Key Legal Frameworks

Nigerian Cybercrime (Prohibition, Prevention, etc.) Act 2015
Nigeria • Financial Institutions • Mandatory

Sections 19 and 37 of the Cybercrimes Act impose explicit obligations on financial institutions:

  • Two people, not one: No single employee may both make and approve a transaction — a basic anti-fraud rule that ensures a second pair of eyes
  • Fraud safeguards: Firms must have proper measures in place to protect customers' sensitive information
  • Verify identity: Customers must be properly identified before being issued cards or devices
  • Know your customer: Firms must confirm who a customer is before carrying out their transfers, payments, or card requests
Penalty: Organizations: fine up to ₦10,000,000. Responsible directors and managers: up to 3 years imprisonment and/or ₦7,000,000 fine. Enforced jointly by law enforcement and the Office of the National Security Adviser.
Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA) / NDPR
Nigeria • All Data Controllers • Mandatory

The Nigeria Data Protection Regulation (now consolidated under the Nigeria Data Protection Act) establishes a comprehensive framework for personal data processing:

  • Data must be collected with the explicit, informed consent of the data subject, with purpose communicated prior to collection
  • Organizations must conduct a detailed privacy and data protection audit within six months of the regulation becoming applicable to them
  • Organizations processing data of Nigerian citizens — regardless of where the organization is geographically based — must comply
  • A Data Protection Officer must be appointed internally or outsourced to a certified firm
  • All organizations must publish a data protection policy aligned with NDPA requirements
  • Data transfers outside Nigeria require NITDA approval, contingent on adequate protection standards in the receiving country
Penalty for breach: The higher of 1–2% of the Data Controller's Annual Gross Revenue, or ₦2,000,000–₦10,000,000, depending on the number of data subjects affected.
General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)
European Union • Applies to any org processing EU data subjects' data • Mandatory

The GDPR applies to any organization processing the personal data of EU residents — irrespective of where the organization is headquartered. For Nigerian financial institutions with EU correspondent banking relationships or EU customers, this has direct compliance implications:

  • Specific and significant rights for data subjects: right to erasure ("right to be forgotten"), right to accurate representation, right to portability, and right to seek compensation
  • Compensation can be sought against both organizations and individual employees
  • Data breach notification to supervisory authorities within 72 hours of becoming aware of a breach
  • Privacy by Design: data protection considerations must be built into systems from inception
Penalty: Up to €20 million or 4% of annual global turnover (whichever is higher) for the most serious violations.
CBN Regulatory Framework & USSD Security Requirements
Nigeria • Financial Institutions • Banking Sector

The Central Bank of Nigeria's regulatory framework for electronic financial services includes mandatory security controls for USSD-based transactions:

  • Implement a proper message authentication mechanism to validate that requests and responses are generated through authenticated users
  • Ensure encryption of USSD information within the processing environment, auditable by regulators
  • Mandate effective second-factor authentication for all customer transactions above ₦20,000

Security Rules for the Global Payments Network (SWIFT)

SWIFT is the network banks use to send money to each other internationally. Any organization connected to it must meet SWIFT's security requirements, designed to keep those payment messages trustworthy. In plain terms, they boil down to six things:

Keep It Separate

Wall off the payment systems from the rest of the company network and the internet.

Reduce Weak Points

Keep software updated and switch off anything not needed, leaving less for attackers to target.

Lock the Doors

Physically protect the payment equipment from unauthorized access.

Protect Passwords

Use strong logins and carefully guard the accounts of the people who operate the system.

Control Access

Give people only the access they need, and keep that access up to date as roles change.

Watch and Respond

Monitor for anything unusual, have a plan to respond, and share warnings with others.

Challenges in Cybercrime Prosecution

Despite an improving legal framework, the prosecution of cybercrime in West Africa faces significant structural challenges that reduce deterrence and allow perpetrators to operate with relative impunity.

01

Evidence Access Barriers

Lack of cooperation from service providers — particularly international platforms — delays or prevents access to the electronic evidence needed to build a prosecutable case.

02

Under-Reporting Culture

Most cybercrimes are not reported. Organizations fear reputational damage; individuals fear stigma or loss of business. Without reports, there is no investigation.

03

Investigative Complexity

Digital forensics requires specialized skills and tools that law enforcement institutions frequently lack, particularly for network-level or cloud-hosted evidence.

04

Mishandled Evidence

Digital evidence has to be collected and handled in very specific ways. If it's mishandled — altered, or its trail not properly documented — a court may throw it out entirely.

05

Witness Reluctance

Witnesses are unwilling to testify, fearing identification by perpetrators, being stigmatized, or losing business relationships with parties implicated in the investigation.

06

Limited Legal Precedent

Cybercrime law is relatively new in the jurisdiction. A limited body of case law means prosecutors and judges lack the settled precedents available in more mature legal domains.

Practical Implication
The current prosecution challenges mean that for many organizations, legal deterrence alone cannot be relied upon to suppress cybercrime threats. A proactive security posture — prevention, detection, and response — remains the primary defense, independent of whether perpetrators face legal consequences.

Why Sharing Warnings Beats Going It Alone

Even the biggest banks are outmatched when they defend alone. Criminals cooperate — they share tools, reuse the same tricks against many targets, and adapt faster than any single organization can keep up with. The most effective answer is for defenders to cooperate too.

When financial firms share what they're seeing, every attack on one becomes an early warning for the rest. Just as banks already share information about credit risk, they can share information about cyber threats — and everyone comes out safer for it.

What Good Intelligence Sharing Looks Like

A strong shared defense rests on three core activities, supported by two things that make them work:

Core 1

Sharing usable warnings — passing on the specific signs of an attack, and details of who's behind it, so others can block it before it reaches them

Core 2

Learning from experience — sharing what worked and what didn't after an incident, so others don't have to learn the same lesson the hard way

Core 3

Staying aware — continuously watching for threat signals, inside and out, to understand what's happening right now

Enabler A

Technology and analytics — using data analysis to spot fraud and predict attack patterns across the whole group

Enabler B

Backing from the top — leadership support, a dedicated team, and a security-aware culture that reaches beyond the IT department

Six Benefits of Sharing

01

Better protection against attacks — when one organization spots an attack, others can block it before it reaches them. One firm's breach becomes everyone else's prevention.

02

Deeper collective knowledge — pooling what different organizations see produces insights no single one could reach alone.

03

A leg-up for smaller players — smaller firms benefit from the tools and expertise of larger members, levelling the playing field.

04

Early word on new threats — a shared community spots new attack campaigns in near real-time, versus the weeks it might take one organization to notice a new pattern alone.

05

Catching attacks that span many targets — some campaigns are only visible once you combine data from several organizations.

06

A safer sector overall — when the whole group is harder to attack, crime becomes less profitable, discouraging future attacks.

What Data Analysis Adds
Applied to shared threat data, analytics can answer four questions: what happened and why, what caused it, what's likely to be attacked next, and what to do about it. Together these turn raw data into decisions.

Recommendations for Organizations

Know Which Rules Apply to You

Work through every law and standard that applies to your organization and check where you fall short. And remember: meeting the legal minimum is the starting point, not the finish line.

Appoint a Data Protection Officer

Data protection law often requires someone to own data privacy. Make it a real role — with direct access to leadership and the authority to stop anything that breaks the rules — not just a title.

Join a Threat-Sharing Group

Industry groups exist specifically to let organizations exchange threat warnings safely and legally. Treat joining one as a security measure in its own right, not an optional extra.

Be Ready to Preserve Evidence

Set up your systems to collect and protect evidence properly before an incident — keep tamper-proof logs and have legal advisers who understand digital evidence on hand. Do this in advance, because you can't recreate it afterwards.

Report Incidents Promptly

Hiding a breach helps no one and often breaks the law. Regulators generally treat honest, early reporting more favourably — and the deadlines are short (often 72 hours), so don't wait.

Never Let One Person Act Alone

Make sure no single individual can both start and approve a financial transaction. It's both a legal requirement and simply good sense — build this “two-person” rule into every financial system and process.

Key Takeaways

  • The law and good security now point the same way. Rules increasingly require the very things good security demands anyway — extra login steps, encryption, response plans, and record-keeping. Complying and being secure have become the same effort.
  • Directors can be held personally responsible. Under these laws, individual leaders — not just the company — can face fines or even prison for security failures. This makes it a boardroom issue, not just an IT one.
  • Don't count on criminals being caught. Prosecuting cybercriminals is genuinely hard, so you can't rely on the justice system to deter them. Preventing attacks yourself is the more dependable strategy.
  • Sharing warnings makes everyone stronger. An organization that both gives and receives threat information effectively gains the eyes and ears of every other member. It costs little and delivers protection no single organization could build alone.
  • Real resilience needs all three: following the law, strong technical defenses, and active cooperation with others. Any one on its own leaves a gap.
