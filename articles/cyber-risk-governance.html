Cyber Risk and the Boardroom: A Leader's Guide to Getting It Right

Why cybersecurity is a leadership responsibility, not just an IT problem — and a practical guide for directors and executives on assessing risk, choosing the right protections, and knowing what to ask.

Joe Smith-Essang Advisory: Financial Sector Clients 2023–2024 Engagements 13 min read
In Plain Terms
Governance here just means the leadership team taking responsibility for cybersecurity — setting the direction, funding it, and keeping an eye on it — rather than leaving it entirely to the IT department. Cyber resilience is the ability to keep running and bounce back quickly when an attack happens. This article is a practical guide for board members and executives who want to ask the right questions and make good decisions, even if they aren't technical.

Why Leadership Has to Own This

Cybersecurity is no longer just an IT issue — it's a core leadership responsibility, the same as financial or legal risk. Organizations that dump it on IT without genuine backing from the top consistently do worse when trouble hits: attacks go unnoticed for longer, cost more to recover from, and bring more trouble with regulators.

This article draws on board-level training and risk assessments delivered to major banks and a national payments operator. It lays out, in plain terms, how to size up your risks, which protections matter most, and what a board should actually do. All client information has been anonymized.

The Core Idea
You can't prevent every attack — and trying to is a losing game. Smart organizations put their energy into two things instead: spotting trouble quickly and limiting the damage when it happens. The goal of good cyber leadership isn't a perfect wall; it's fast, well-practised recovery.

How to Assess Your Cyber Risk

A risk assessment isn't a one-off audit — it's an ongoing process of working out what could go wrong, how bad it would be, and what to do about it. The approach below, used with a national payments operator, runs in six repeating steps.

01

Asset Identification

Catalogue all organizational assets: physical infrastructure (servers, network devices, workstations), digital data (intellectual property, sensitive customer information), and human assets (staff with elevated access). Prioritize assets by sensitivity and business criticality — not all assets warrant the same level of protection investment.

02

Threat Assessment

For each asset class, identify applicable threats — both internal (malicious insiders, negligent staff, contractors) and external (organized crime, nation states, hacktivists, skilled individual hackers). Evaluate threat likelihood by considering attacker motivation, skill level, and the organization's current security posture. Not all threats are equally probable for all organizations.

03

Vulnerability Assessment

Systematically identify weaknesses that threats could exploit: weak passwords, outdated software, misconfigurations, insufficient physical security, and lack of security awareness training. Conduct both automated vulnerability scans and manual penetration testing. Assess the potential damage of each vulnerability being exploited, not just its existence.

04

Risk Analysis

Quantify risk by combining threat likelihood and vulnerability impact. Risk = Likelihood × Impact. Use risk matrices to classify findings by priority tier. This scoring drives resource allocation — high-risk findings demand immediate capital and attention; low-risk findings may be monitored or accepted. Allocate resources based on risk priorities, not organizational politics.

05

Risk Mitigation

Put protections in place across three areas: technical (firewalls, intrusion detection, encryption, device protection); administrative (access rules, response plans, staff training); and physical (locks, barriers, controlled entry to server rooms). Check regularly that these are actually working, and adjust as threats change.

06

Monitoring & Review

Continuously monitor network traffic, system logs, security alerts, and vulnerability scan outputs. Conduct penetration tests on a defined cadence. Periodically review and update the risk assessment as the threat landscape and organizational environment change — a risk assessment that is not updated is not an asset; it is a false sense of security.

The Five Areas of Protection to Get Right

Across financial-sector work, the same five areas kept coming up as the foundation of a strong security setup. Each one blocks a different way in for attackers. You don't need to understand the technology in depth — just make sure each area is genuinely covered.

1. Controlling Who Gets In

  • Require a second login step (not just a password)
  • Give people access based on their job role
  • Grant everyone the least access they need to do their work
  • Set clear rules for who can open which files and systems
  • Keep the most powerful accounts separate and closely watched

2. Protecting the Network

  • Divide the network into sections so a problem can't spread everywhere
  • Use a modern firewall, kept up to date
  • Have systems that detect and block intruders
  • Make remote access secure, with a second login step
  • Pull security alerts into one place so nothing is missed

3. Protecting the Data

  • Encrypt data both where it's stored and while it's moving
  • Use tools that stop sensitive data leaving the organization
  • Follow the 3-2-1 backup rule: three copies, on two types of media, one kept off-site
  • Regularly test that backups actually restore
  • Know what data you have before trying to protect it

4. Responding and Recovering

  • Have a written response plan with clear roles
  • Have a plan to keep the business running through a disruption
  • Have a plan to restore IT systems and data after a disaster
  • Practise these plans regularly, not just file them away
  • Review every incident afterwards and apply the lessons

5. Training Your People

  • Run practice phishing tests every quarter
  • Teach staff the common tricks scammers use to manipulate them
  • Train people on how to report a problem, tailored to their role
  • Encourage good password habits and provide a password manager
  • Set clear rules for staff using personal phones for work

What the Board Should Actually Do

Good oversight is more than reading a quarterly IT report. Boards need to treat cyber risk as seriously as financial risk — asking questions, setting expectations, and making sure it's properly funded.

What the Board Should Make Sure Of

Someone is clearly accountable for cybersecurity
It's properly funded and given real attention
Cyber risk is reviewed by the board regularly
Legal and regulatory requirements are being met
Key suppliers are checked for good security too
Independent security tests happen on a schedule
The board gets clear reports on the risks that matter most
Leaders visibly take security seriously, setting the example
The organization follows a recognized security standard
The organization can attract and keep skilled security staff
Serious incidents are reported to and reviewed by the board
There's a plan to keep the business running through a crisis

Four Risks That Deserve Board Attention

Working with major financial institutions, four risks came up again and again as ones leadership needs to watch:

  • Suppliers and partners: They're often the weakest link. Check their security before signing on, put response-time commitments in contracts, and never automatically trust a supplier's access — verify it.
  • Your own people: Staff and executives are prime targets for having their passwords stolen. Require a second login step, train people, and build a culture where they report mistakes without fear of blame.
  • Aging or misconfigured technology: Old systems and sloppy setups create openings. Test regularly for weaknesses, keep software patched, and set secure defaults for anything in the cloud.
  • Legal and regulatory exposure: Rules keep changing, and falling behind brings fines. Use tools to track compliance, assign someone to own it, and check regularly for gaps.

Numbers the Board Should Ask For

You don't need to be technical to hold a security team accountable — you just need to know which numbers to ask about and what “good” looks like.

Time to Notice an Attack

How long from an attack starting to someone noticing it. For a major institution, aim for under 24 hours.

Time to Shut It Down

How long from noticing an attack to stopping it. Shows how well the response team actually performs.

Successful Attacks

How many incidents each quarter actually caused harm — stolen data, financial loss, or downtime.

Data Breaches

Confirmed cases of data being accessed without permission. The trend over time matters more than any single number.

Phishing Test Failure Rate

The share of staff who fall for practice phishing emails. Aim to get this under 5% within a year of training.

Time to Fix Known Weaknesses

How quickly serious software flaws get patched once they're discovered.

Supplier-Related Incidents

How many security incidents came through a supplier or partner rather than your own systems.

Standards Compliance

How fully you meet the security standards that apply to you, based on your most recent audit.

Practising for a Crisis: Tabletop Exercises

A written response plan isn't enough on its own — it has to be tested before a real crisis, not during one. A “tabletop exercise” is a walk-through where the key people (IT, legal, communications, executives, and board members) talk through a realistic attack scenario together, to find the gaps in the plan and in who decides what.

 Scenario 1 — Key Vendor Service Disruption

Setup

A critical third-party vendor providing essential IT services experiences a major service disruption. This results in significant operational impacts: system outages and data access limitations across core business functions.

 Scenario 2 — Technology Failure or Core System Outage

Setup

A critical technology system essential for core operations experiences a major outage. Customer-facing services are disrupted — online platforms, payment systems, and branch operations are all affected. The cause is unknown at onset.

The most useful exercises probe questions that rarely get tested: Who has the authority to declare a crisis and activate the backup plan? Who talks to regulators, and how quickly? Who steps in if the security chief is unavailable? What does the board need to know, and when?

How to Run a Good Exercise
Run it under time pressure, like the real thing — not as a relaxed chat. Throw in surprises partway through (a key person is unreachable; the backup system is also affected) to see whether the plan holds up when things don't go to script.

Recommendations for Organizations

Follow a Recognized Standard

Adopt a well-known security standard as your backbone. It gives you structure, lets you compare yourself to others, and shows regulators and customers you're taking the right steps.

Put Cyber on the Board Agenda

Review cyber risk at board level at least quarterly. Cover how the key numbers are trending, any incidents, gaps, and new regulations. Boards that get clear, regular reporting make better decisions.

Manage Supplier Risk Properly

Ask significant suppliers to prove their security, and put response-time commitments in their contracts. Remember: a break-in through a supplier is still your problem.

Practise Your Response Every Year

Walk through a realistic crisis at least once a year, with people from across the business including board members. Write down the gaps you find and fix them before the next round.

Invest in Your People

Skilled security staff are hard to find and keep. Grow your own through training and fair pay, and bring in outside specialists for roles you can't fill internally.

Test Recovery, Not Just Defense

Most organizations test how well they keep attackers out; few test how well they recover. Practising getting back up and running is just as important — because sooner or later, something will get through.

Key Takeaways

  • Assessing risk isn't the same as managing it. A completed checklist means nothing if it doesn't lead to action. Keep assessing, and let it drive real improvements.
  • Your weakest area defines your security. Great technology with untrained staff still leaves you exposed. All five areas — access, network, data, response, and people — need attention together.
  • Informed leaders lead better. Organizations with engaged, well-briefed boards invest more wisely, respond more decisively, and recover faster. Board engagement measurably improves resilience.
  • A supplier's breach is your breach. The fines, damage, and disruption land on you regardless of whose system was compromised first.
  • Recovery, not perfection, is the goal. No one can stop every attack. What sets a mature organization apart is how quickly and calmly it detects, contains, and recovers.
