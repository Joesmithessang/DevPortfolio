Critical Infrastructure Under Siege:
The 2024 Cyber Threat Intelligence Landscape

A plain-language look at who was attacking essential services — banks, hospitals, power, telecoms — in 2024, why they were doing it, how the attacks worked, and the practical steps organizations can take to defend themselves.

2024 Research 12 min read Joe Smith-Essang Threat Intelligence

Scope & Methodology

This analysis draws on real consulting work, publicly available information, official cyber-authority advisories, and commercial threat-intelligence services. All client-identifying information has been anonymized, and the recommendations apply to any organization, anywhere.

In Plain Terms

Critical infrastructure means the services a country can't function without — banks, hospitals, electricity, water, phone and internet networks. Because knocking these out causes maximum disruption, they're prime targets. In 2024, three kinds of attackers stood out: government-backed spies gathering secrets, ransomware gangs in it for money, and insiders who abused the access they'd been trusted with. This article explains who they were and what actually protects against them.

The Big Picture

In 2024, attacks on essential services were driven by three forces coming together: political conflict spilling into cyberspace, ransomware becoming a slick, money-making industry, and attackers increasingly breaking in through the smaller suppliers their targets depend on. Government-backed hacking groups pushed beyond their usual spying targets into banks, energy companies, and telecoms networks. At the same time, ransomware gangs — run with the efficiency of real businesses — locked up victims and stole their data at scale.

Three types of attacker did most of the damage: government-backed groups after long-term intelligence; ransomware gangs buying their way in and demanding payment; and insiders — staff or contractors who abused access they already had, often because no one was watching the powerful accounts closely enough.

When Real-World Conflicts Spill Into Cyberspace

Cyberattacks are now a standard tool in political conflicts — they're cheap, deniable, and can hit an enemy from the other side of the world. Three global flashpoints shaped the attacks seen in the region during 2024:

RUSSIA-UKRAINE CONFLICT

Russian hacking groups reached beyond the war zone to countries seen as supporting Ukraine, including African financial and government bodies. Investigators saw signs of preparation for sabotage, including malware built to permanently wipe systems rather than just steal from them.

ISRAEL-HAMAS CONFLICT

Activist hacking groups and Iran-linked attackers stepped up their operations against banks, media, and telecoms companies worldwide — including flooding websites with fake traffic to knock them offline (known as a denial-of-service attack).

REGIONAL INSTABILITY

Political upheaval and weak oversight created openings for hackers-for-hire working on behalf of private clients. Energy and mining operations became targets of campaigns aimed at causing economic damage.

Highest-Risk Sectors in 2024

Based on incident frequency, impact scores, and regulatory exposure, the five highest-risk sectors were Banking & Finance, Government Services, ICT/Telecoms, Health Services, and Energy. These sectors collectively accounted for over 70% of significant incidents observed during the analysis period.

Three Real Incidents, Anonymized

These short case studies are based on real events, with all identifying details removed. Each one shows a different kind of attack — and, more usefully, the specific gap that let it succeed.

Insider Financial Fraud — $29M Loss at a Major Financial Institution

SECTOR: Banking & Finance  |  THREAT TYPE: Insider Threat  |  IMPACT: $29M+

A trusted employee at a large bank used their high-level access to the core banking system to quietly divert funds over a long period, working with an outside criminal group to move the money abroad. The key problem: the same person could both start and approve a transaction. When one individual controls both steps, there's no second pair of eyes to catch fraud.

What let it happen: no system watching for unusual staff behaviour; one role that combined the power to both initiate and approve payments; and no regular review of who had access to what.

Patient Data Stolen — Healthcare Technology Provider

SECTOR: Health Services  |  THREAT TYPE: External Breach  |  IMPACT: Patient records exposed

A health-technology startup that stored patient records was breached, exposing sensitive personal and medical information. The data showed up for sale on the dark web within three days — so fast that the attacker had almost certainly lined up a buyer in advance. The way in was a piece of the system that connects apps together (an “API”) that had a known flaw and weak login checks.

What let it happen: weak security on that app connection; no security testing before the system went live; no dark web monitoring that could have raised the alarm sooner; and failure to meet data protection law.

Ransomware Shuts Down a Government Office

SECTOR: Government Services  |  THREAT TYPE: Ransomware (FunkSec)  |  IMPACT: Days of downtime

A regional government's systems were locked up by the FunkSec ransomware gang. Before locking everything, the attackers first stole citizen records and internal data, then published samples online to prove it and pressure officials into paying. Public services were down for several days during recovery.

What let it happen: out-of-date protection on old computers; a “flat” network with no internal walls, so the attack spread freely once inside; no rehearsed plan for a ransomware attack; and backups that were all online — so they got locked too.

Who's Doing the Attacking

Eight groups came up again and again in 2024. The table below is a quick reference for security teams; if the codenames don't mean much to you, focus on the Type column, which tells you the group's motive:

  • APT (Advanced Persistent Threat) — well-resourced, usually government-backed groups that break in quietly and stay for a long time, mostly to spy.
  • Ransomware — criminals who lock up systems and demand payment.
  • BEC (Business Email Compromise) — fraudsters who impersonate executives or suppliers by email to trick staff into sending money.
  • Hacktivist — politically motivated groups who attack to make a statement or cause disruption.

FunkSec: A Sign of Things to Come

FunkSec appeared in late 2024 and stood out for reportedly using artificial intelligence to help write its malware. Parts of their code looked machine-generated rather than hand-written. The worrying trend here is that AI lowers the skill needed to create attack software — meaning more people can now do damage that once required expert programmers. Expect more of this.

The Key Pattern

Nearly every major weakness in 2024 was in the equipment that sits at the “front door” of the network — the remote-access systems (VPNs), firewalls, and their control panels that connect a company to the internet. These are exactly the devices that many security tools don't watch closely. Keeping this front-door equipment patched and up to date deserves leadership attention, not just routine IT maintenance.

How to Rank What to Protect First

You can't fix everything at once, so you need a way to decide what matters most. These simple formulas give a repeatable way to score risk and business impact — turning a gut feeling into a number you can compare across systems and defend in a budget conversation.

RISK = HOW LIKELY × HOW EXPOSED, MINUS YOUR DEFENSES

Risk = ( T × V ) ÷ C

T = Threat (how capable and motivated the attackers are)  |  V = Vulnerability (how exposed you are)  |  C = Controls (how good your defenses are). Stronger defenses lower the risk.

BUSINESS IMPACT: FIVE THINGS, WEIGHTED BY WHAT HURTS MOST

Impact = ( BD×0.30 ) + ( DS×0.25 ) + ( FL×0.20 ) + ( RC×0.15 ) + ( RD×0.10 )

BD = Business Disruption  |  DS = Data Sensitivity  |  FL = Financial Loss
RC = Regulatory & Compliance exposure  |  RD = Reputational Damage

In plain terms: score each system on these five things, but give more weight to the ones that hurt most. Business disruption counts for the most (30%) because when an essential service goes down, the knock-on effects reach far beyond the organization itself. Data sensitivity comes next (25%), because leaking personal data brings both fines and lasting reputational damage.

The Rules Are Tightening

Governments are steadily raising the legal bar for protecting essential services. If you operate in or supply one of these sectors, expect more rules, not fewer:

  • Data protection laws: Many countries now require you to report a breach within 72 hours, with fines up to 2% of annual revenue for failing to comply.
  • Tougher cybercrime laws: New laws create specific, heavier penalties for attacking essential services, and make reporting incidents mandatory.
  • Minimum standards by sector: Financial regulators now require licensed institutions to have a security monitoring team, modern threat-detection tools on their devices, and a tested plan for responding to incidents.
  • Industrial system rules: Energy and utility regulators are starting to require security checks of the systems that run physical equipment (like power stations), after real intrusions were discovered.

Seven Ways to Defend Essential Services

These are the main lessons from the analysis above, written as practical advice for any organization that runs or supplies an essential service:

01

Go Looking for Threats — and Share What You Learn

Don't just wait for an alarm to go off. Actively search your own systems for signs of the attack methods described here. Sign up to government cyber-authority alerts and join industry groups that share warnings. Knowing about an attack before it reaches you changes everything.

02

Patch Your Internet-Facing Equipment First

The remote-access systems, firewalls, and their control panels that face the internet are attackers' favourite way in. Update them within a day or two of a serious flaw being announced, keep their control panels off the public internet, and require a second login step (MFA) everywhere. This was the single clearest pattern of 2024.

03

Guard Against Insiders

Make sure no single person can both start and approve an important action (like a large payment). Review who has access to what every few months, watch for unusual behaviour on sensitive systems, and keep tight control of the most powerful accounts. The $29M insider fraud above was entirely preventable with these basic steps.

04

Be Ready to Survive Ransomware

Keep at least one set of backups completely offline, so it can't be locked. Put modern protection on every device. Divide your network into sections so one infected computer can't reach everything — especially your backups. Practise your response once a year, and agree in advance how you'd decide whether to pay or recover — before it happens, not during.

05

Protect the Systems That Run Physical Equipment

The computers that control machinery — in power plants, factories, or building systems — are increasingly targeted and are often far less protected than office IT. Know exactly what you have, keep these systems separate from the office network, and monitor them for anything unusual. Attackers target them precisely because they can cause real-world, physical disruption.

06

Use Compliance to Fund Good Security

Legal deadlines are useful leverage. Use required standards to win budget for security work that might otherwise be turned down. Line your security plans up with recognized standards (such as ISO 27001 or the NIST framework), and treat audits as a health check — not just a box-ticking chore.

07

Beat Scams with Culture, Not Just Tools

Tricking staff with fake emails (phishing) is still the most common way in for both spies and ransomware gangs. Email filters and a second login step help, but they can't catch everything. Train your people, run practice phishing tests to see who's at risk, and — crucially — thank staff for reporting suspicious messages rather than punishing those who slip up.

Six Things to Do This Quarter

If you want to act on all this now, here are six concrete starting points:

Check Your Internet-Facing Equipment

Make a list of every remote-access system, firewall, and similar device that faces the internet. Check each against the known 2024 flaws and update them within 48 hours.

Review Who Has Powerful Access

List everyone who can administer systems, manage databases, or approve financial transactions. Remove access that isn't needed, and require a second login step for all the accounts that remain.

Test That Your Backups Actually Restore

Run a restore test this quarter. Confirm at least one backup is kept offline, and check that you can bring systems back within the time your business can tolerate being down.

Sign Up for Threat Warnings

Make sure your team receives alerts from your national cyber authority and at least one threat-information service for your industry, so you hear about active attacks early.

Run a Ransomware Practice Drill

Spend a day walking your team through a realistic ransomware scenario. Test your plan, your communications, and your decision-making. Find the gaps before an attacker does.

Watch the Dark Web

Since stolen data can appear for sale within three days of a breach, set up dark web monitoring for your email addresses and sensitive names. Our companion article explains how.

Key Takeaways

  • Global politics is now part of your risk. Conflicts on the other side of the world create real cyber threats to local services. Your planning has to take that into account.
  • Your internet-facing equipment is the front line. Every major 2024 weakness was in remote-access systems and firewalls. These should be your first priority to patch.
  • Insider threats come down to weak oversight. Splitting duties, reviewing access regularly, and watching for unusual behaviour aren't optional extras — they're the controls that stop insider fraud.
  • Ransomware gangs run like businesses. Beat their efficiency with your own discipline: offline backups, modern device protection, a divided network, and a rehearsed plan.
  • AI is now used by both sides. Attackers are starting to use AI to build malware faster. Defenders will increasingly need AI-powered tools just to keep up.
